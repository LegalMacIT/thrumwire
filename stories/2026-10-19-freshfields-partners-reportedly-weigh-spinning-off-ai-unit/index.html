Freshfields partners reportedly weigh spinning off AI unit Freshfields Lab as a separate, investable company

About this briefing: researched and written by Claude, Anthropic's AI assistant, for Thrum. Selected for publication by Hector A. Cruz. Facts are from the linked source. About Thrum

A top-tier law firm is reportedly considering turning its in-house AI lab into an outside-funded business that could be worth billions.

  • The Financial Times reported on 14 October 2026 that senior Freshfields partners had discussed making Freshfields Lab a separate company.
  • The company could be opened to external investors.
  • The unit builds AI tools for the firm's lawyers and clients.
  • The senior partners reportedly believe it could be worth billions.
  • This is a discussion reported by the FT, not a confirmed decision. The original FT article was not reviewed, and the secondary report gave no deal terms.

Freshfields is reported to be considering a spinoff of Freshfields Lab, its in-house legal technology unit. The unit would become a separate company that could take outside capital. The report, relayed by PYMNTS from the Financial Times, describes senior-partner discussions only. No decision has been announced.

The idea tests whether a law firm's AI work can be valued as a technology business as well as a cost centre. Law firm ownership rules have traditionally limited outside investment in the practice itself. A separate technology company is one way to bring in capital without changing the partnership structure. The report did not cover how conflicts, client data and the independence of legal advice would be handled, and those points were not available in the source reviewed.

The move would also change the competitive picture. Firms that build their own AI would be competing with the vendors they currently buy from, and could become vendors to other firms and clients.

For legal-technology advisers: Clients will probably ask whether to build, buy or commercialise AI tools. Advisers should be prepared to assess the costs of a proprietary build and the dependency risks if a firm's tooling moves into a separately owned company. They should also check how data-use and confidentiality terms would change if a tool's owner became an investor-backed entity. The report is unconfirmed, so advice should treat it as a signal and not a fact.

For lawyers deploying AI: Lawyers using a firm's in-house tools should find out who owns the tools, where client data is processed, and whether outside investors could gain access to usage data or work product. Any shift to an external structure would raise questions about client consent, conflicts and professional-secrecy duties that should be raised with firm management before client matters are put through such tools.

Source: PYMNTS (citing the Financial Times) · 2026-10-14 · United Kingdom / Global
