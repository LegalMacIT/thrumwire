NVIDIA releases Nemotron 3.5 Bolt E4B, a 4GB Gemma-4-based small model for local conversational agents

About this briefing: researched and written by Claude, Anthropic's AI assistant, for Thrum. Selected for publication by Hector A. Cruz. Facts are from the linked source. About Thrum

NVIDIA's launch post on X drew about 268,000 views, and Scale AI's MultiChallenge leaderboard result put the model 12.8 points ahead of the Gemma 4 E4B model it was built from.

  • NVIDIA released Nemotron 3.5 Bolt E4B 4GB on 23 October 2026. NVIDIA describes it as an open small language model for conversational agents, voice and device assistants, interactive characters and robotics or IoT interfaces.
  • The Hugging Face model card lists 8B total parameters (4B effective). It was post-trained from Gemma-4-E4B-it, ships in Q4_K_M precision and has a 64K-token input and output context. The weights are at huggingface.co/nvidia/NVIDIA-Nemotron-3.5-Bolt-E4B-4GB.
  • NVIDIA claims roughly 40% higher average quality than its prior model on conversational-agent benchmarks and up to about 80% fewer output tokens than peers. The benchmarks are MultiChallenge, RoleMRC, BFCL-v3, IHEval, SysBench and RelyToolBench.
  • Scale AI's MultiChallenge leaderboard scored the model at 56.03%, ahead of Gemma 4 E4B at 43.2%. This figure was relayed through NVIDIA's own X thread.
  • NVIDIA says weights, data and recipes are open and customisable. The licence text was not visible in the excerpts reviewed, and the card lists llama.cpp deployment as 'recipe pending'.

NVIDIA has added a very small member to its Nemotron 3.5 family. Bolt E4B takes Google's Gemma 4 E4B and applies Nemotron post-training aimed at multi-turn tool use, instruction following and grounding in the supplied input. The result is a model that NVIDIA says fits in about 4GB. It targets RTX, Jetson and DGX Spark hardware, and an accompanying demo ran it locally on a Jetson Orin Nano 8GB. An NVIDIA blog post indicates that it is the third model in a family that also includes the 120B Super VL and the 74B-A9B Bolt, which were released earlier in October.

The headline numbers come from NVIDIA's own technical report and benchmark tables. These compare Bolt E4B with Nemotron 3 Nano 4B, Qwen 3.5 4B and Gemma 4 E4B. The one external-looking figure, Scale AI's MultiChallenge score of 56.03% against Gemma 4 E4B's 43.2%, was reported through NVIDIA's own channels. Independent replication had not been located at the time of writing. The model's lead is also specific to conversational and tool-use tests, not to general knowledge or coding.

For law firms and professional-services buyers, the item matters mainly as a data point on small, locally runnable models. A model of this size can run on a single workstation or edge device, so client data need not leave the machine. That is the usual reason firms look at open weights. The trade-off is that the capability ceiling is far below that of the frontier models firms usually license. Another open question is the licensing chain. The model derives from Gemma and is released by NVIDIA, and the licence terms were not confirmed in the sources reviewed.

For legal-technology advisers: Points to watch are the exact licence that governs a Gemma-derived NVIDIA release, including any use restrictions that pass through from the base model. Also worth watching are whether independent evaluators reproduce the MultiChallenge and tool-use results, and how the 'recipe pending' status of llama.cpp support resolves. Questions worth asking vendors and IT teams: which inference runtime is supported, what hardware is needed for acceptable latency, and whether any vendor is embedding this model in a product and how it is attributed and patched.

For lawyers deploying AI: What to watch is whether a 4B-effective model is reliable enough for any client-facing or document-facing task, since the published benchmarks measure conversation and tool calling rather than legal accuracy. Running locally can reduce confidentiality exposure, but supervision and verification duties still apply to outputs. Questions worth asking include where the model runs and who controls updates, whether prompts and logs are retained on the device, and how outputs are checked before reliance, given that the benchmark claims come from the vendor.
