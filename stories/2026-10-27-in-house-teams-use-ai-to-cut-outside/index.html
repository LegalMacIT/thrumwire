In-house teams use AI to cut outside-counsel work, pushing law firms toward passing on savings and alternative fees

About this briefing: researched and written by Claude, Anthropic's AI assistant, for Thrum. Selected for publication by Hector A. Cruz. Facts are from the linked source. About Thrum

The article points to a shift in client bargaining power. It cites a report that most legal departments are already trimming or plan to trim outside-counsel assignments because of AI.

  • In-house teams are using generative AI to do more legal work internally, cutting the work they send to law firms.
  • One report cited by Law.com found more than half of in-house teams had already decreased assignments to outside counsel. Nearly all legal departments plan to do so within a year.
  • Law firms are responding by passing along AI cost savings and offering alternative billing structures.
  • Law.com reported that complicated, high-stakes work will likely remain fruitful for law firms.

Law.com Legal Tech News reports that in-house teams are using generative AI to take on more legal work themselves. This follows years of speculation about how AI would change the firm-client relationship. A report cited in the article found that more than half of in-house teams have already reduced work sent to outside counsel.

Law firms are reportedly responding with AI-driven savings passed on to clients and with alternative billing structures. The article's outlook is that complex, high-stakes matters will stay with firms while routine work moves in-house. The name of the report, its sample size and the exact figures were not available in the portions of the article reviewed.

The item matters because the demand side of AI adoption is now visible. Firm AI rollouts have been framed as efficiency tools. Here, client AI use is itself reshaping pricing and workload. That adds context to current vendor moves toward in-house buyers, such as intake products and Microsoft's Copilot for Legal, which other legal-technology coverage this month discussed.

For legal-technology advisers: What to watch is whether clients' AI tooling begins to drive outside-counsel guidelines, billing-data requirements and expectations of AI-enabled pricing. Questions worth asking include how firms track matter-level AI efficiencies and whether pricing and time-capture systems can support alternative fee models.

For lawyers deploying AI: Questions include how efficiency gains will be reflected in client billing and how firms disclose AI use to clients. It is also worth asking how work handed back from in-house AI tools is reviewed and who is accountable for its accuracy.
