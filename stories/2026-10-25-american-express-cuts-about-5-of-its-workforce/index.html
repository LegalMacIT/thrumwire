American Express cuts about 5% of its workforce, roughly 3,800 jobs, as AI reshapes payments

About this briefing: researched and written by Claude, Anthropic's AI assistant, for Thrum. Selected for publication by Hector A. Cruz. Facts are from the linked source. About Thrum

A highly profitable company announced thousands of job cuts while its CEO talked about AI both as a productivity tool and as a threat to the payments model.

  • AmEx announced on Friday 23 October that it is cutting 5% of its global workforce, about 3,800 jobs. It had about 76,800 employees at the end of 2025.
  • The one-time restructuring cost was $336 million. Third-quarter profit still rose 28% to $3.7 billion.
  • CEO Stephen Squeri described the aim as a 'more agile and efficient' organisation, and the cuts are linked to a simpler structure and faster decision-making.
  • Squeri said AmEx is using AI to boost productivity and automate processes. He said 'agentic transaction volumes are still small, but the direction is clear'.
  • He also said AmEx is taking a 'risk-based approach' to AI, to guard against rogue agents. The company gave AFP no further details on the restructuring.

AmEx announced the restructuring alongside strong third-quarter earnings, with revenue up 9% to $20.1 billion. This is a cut made from a position of profitability. The company's own explanation centres on agility, simplification and capacity to invest, not on AI-driven replacement of roles.

The AI link is indirect but present. Squeri said AmEx is using AI to automate processes. He also pointed to agentic AI as a force changing how consumers discover and pay for things. The restructuring therefore reads as a response to AI-driven change in the market as well as an internal efficiency move. The sources reviewed did not tie specific roles or the 3,800 figure to particular AI deployments.

For professional-services and enterprise-technology audiences, the item adds to a run of financial-sector announcements, including Barclays, in which AI is cited as context for large reductions. Its mention of rogue agents is a reminder that risk governance for autonomous AI is entering executive earnings commentary. It signals how financial-sector clients may frame AI risk when they brief advisers and suppliers.

For legal-technology advisers: Worth watching is how AmEx and similar firms define a 'risk-based approach' to agentic AI, and whether that becomes contract language for suppliers. Questions worth asking vendors include what controls exist for agent actions, what audit logs are kept, and how liability is allocated when an agent acts incorrectly. Which functions are cut in the restructuring was not disclosed in the sources reviewed.

For lawyers deploying AI: Worth watching is how agentic payments and AI-agent transactions are treated in client advisory work, including questions of authority, consent and liability. Questions worth asking include how supervision duties apply when AI tools take actions rather than only draft text. Another is how restructuring at financial clients may change who instructs counsel and what AI-related terms they require.
