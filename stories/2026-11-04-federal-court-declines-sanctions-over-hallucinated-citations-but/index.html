Federal court declines sanctions over hallucinated citations but refuses to consider replacement authorities

About this briefing: researched and written by Claude, Anthropic's AI assistant, for Thrum. Selected for publication by Hector A. Cruz. Facts are from the linked source. About Thrum

The ruling is a counterpoint to harsher recent sanctions. It shows a court accepting candour and corrections while still penalising the filing.

  • A motion to dismiss filed by defendant Cutera cited cases the court could not locate.
  • The court issued an order to show cause on October 22, 2026, then discharged it after reviewing counsel's response.
  • The court cited counsel's explanation, acknowledgment of the errors and submission of corrections in declining sanctions at that time.
  • The court disregarded the inaccurate citations and said it would not consider the replacement authorities offered in counsel's declaration.
  • The court warned that further fabricated or materially inaccurate authorities may bring sanctions after notice.

The court in Farhat v. Cutera treated the hallucinated citations in a motion to dismiss as an honest error. It discharged its show-cause order without sanctions. According to the EDRM write-up, the decision relied on counsel's explanation, acknowledgment and corrections. The court still admonished counsel that they remain responsible for the accuracy of their filings.

The practical penalty was that the court would not consider the replacement authorities. Counsel therefore could not repair the motion by substituting new cases after the fact. The court also warned that any repeat could draw sanctions. The EDRM post did not say whether generative AI was formally confirmed as the source, and the underlying order was not reviewed beyond the excerpts quoted there.

For firms and enterprise buyers, the case shows that prompt disclosure can mitigate sanctions but does not remove the substantive cost of a defective filing. It adds to the body of decisions on AI-related citation failures that malpractice insurers, general counsel and clients may track.

For legal-technology advisers: Questions worth asking include whether citation-checking features in vendor tools log verification, and whether firms keep audit trails that could support a candid response to a show-cause order. Also worth watching is whether verification workflows catch errors before filing.

For lawyers deploying AI: Points to watch include how courts weigh prompt acknowledgment against the filing's defects, and what supervision exists over drafts prepared with AI. Another open question is how quickly a firm can document its verification process if the court asks for an explanation.
